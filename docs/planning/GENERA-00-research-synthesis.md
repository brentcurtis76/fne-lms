# Building GENERA: a research synthesis across ten design questions

**No existing platform integrates individual student development, classroom social dynamics, and early warning detection across the full school journey — making GENERA a genuinely novel system.** This finding, confirmed across analysis of over 20 platforms and dozens of research sources, means the design team cannot simply copy an existing model. Instead, GENERA must draw selectively from developmental psychology, sociometric methodology, inclusive education research, positive psychology, and pedagogical traditions spanning five continents to build something fundamentally new. What follows is a question-by-question synthesis of findings, evidence, and actionable design implications for a platform serving Chilean students from early childhood through graduation.

---

## 1. No platform does all three things at once

The market for student support technology is deeply fragmented. **Panorama Education** comes closest to integration, combining SEL surveys, attendance/behavior/coursework indicators, and early warning dashboards into unified student profiles. An IES/REL Mid-Atlantic study with DC Public Schools validated its approach, and Panorama claims **80% prediction accuracy for ninth-grade course failures using sixth-grade data**, meeting ESSA Level II evidence standards. However, Panorama does not map peer relationships, lacks early childhood modules, and operates exclusively in English for the US market.

**DESSA (Aperture Education)** excels at strength-based SEL assessment across K-12, with **130+ peer-reviewed studies** and national norming. A Johns Hopkins/City Year study of 38,000+ students found one tier of growth in DESSA skills correlates with one year of academic gains, and students without foundational resilience are **4.5x more likely to be suspended**. Connecticut adopted DESSA statewide. Yet DESSA measures individual competencies, not social structures or peer dynamics.

**Branching Minds** leads in MTSS (Multi-Tiered System of Supports), managing nearly **9 million support plans annually** with integrated academic, behavioral, and SEL data. Its standout feature for GENERA: student records "follow the student across campuses and years, including key academic transition points." But it lacks sociometric mapping, family co-creation features, and operates within a US-centric MTSS framework.

**Storypark** (New Zealand, 11,000+ centers, 37 countries) offers the closest model for GENERA's early childhood layer: parent-owned lifetime digital portfolios with rich narrative "learning stories." Parents retain free access even after leaving a center. But Storypark serves only early childhood and lacks any early warning or social dynamics capability.

**ClassDojo** demonstrates the power of simplicity — used in **95% of US schools**, reaching 51+ million students — but scholarly critique (Williamson 2017, Bradbury 2019, Manolev 2019) documents how its binary reward/punishment system reduces complex social-emotional development to behaviorist data points and creates surveillance dynamics.

In Chile specifically, **no comprehensive platform exists** in this space. MINEDUC operates administrative systems (SIGE, Ayuda Mineduc, DEMRE), and the Agencia de Calidad tracks school-level climate indicators, but no Chilean tool integrates developmental tracking, relational mapping, and early warning for individual students. IntegratePIE manages PIE compliance documentation but does not touch student development holistically. This gap represents GENERA's core market opportunity.

**Design implication:** GENERA should adopt Panorama's multi-indicator student profile model, DESSA's strength-based assessment philosophy, Branching Minds' longitudinal continuity architecture, and Storypark's parent-owned narrative approach — but integrate these with the classroom social dynamics mapping that none of these platforms attempt. The sociogram is GENERA's most distinctive feature and the biggest unmet need in the market.

## 2. How agency and self-awareness evolve — and what this means for the interface

Developmental psychology provides a remarkably clear map for how GENERA's interface and interaction model must shift across stages. The research converges from multiple traditions.

**Ages 3–6 (Educación Parvularia):** Piaget's preoperational stage means children think egocentrically and cannot reliably take others' perspectives. Erikson's initiative-versus-guilt crisis means they explore purposefully but cannot self-evaluate. Kuhn (2000) established that by age 3, children distinguish thinking from perceiving and achieve false-belief understanding by age 4 — but executive control remains sharply limited. Flavell, Friedrichs & Hoyt's (1970) classic study showed preschoolers who claimed they were "ready" to recall items then failed at recall, demonstrating that **young children cannot accurately monitor their own learning**. Harter & Pike (1984) showed children ages 4-7 do not differentiate cognitive from physical competence — self-views collapse onto a single, overwhelmingly positive factor. A systematic review in Quality of Life Research (2021) concluded children under 5 cannot validly self-report complex internal states. Platform implication: at this stage, collect data through behavioral observation and educator/parent proxy reports. The interface should use large touch targets (≥2cm per Nielsen Norman Group guidelines), full voice narration for pre-readers, binary emotional check-ins (happy/sad faces only), and 2-3 large visual activity choices supporting Erikson's initiative stage.

**Ages 6–10 (1er Ciclo Básica):** Piaget's concrete operational stage brings logical thinking, classification, and decentered attention. Erikson's industry-versus-inferiority crisis makes competence feedback critical. By age 8, children begin accurately monitoring their own memory and learning (Wellman, 1978). **Self-report becomes substantially reliable from age 8 onward** — Varni et al.'s PedsQL study of 8,591 children confirms this, and Conijn et al. (2020, Assessment) developed validity indices showing self-report quality improves linearly with age. Harter documented that around age 8, self-evaluations become more realistic and social comparison replaces temporal self-comparison. Platform implication: introduce structured self-reporting around age 8 with 3-point pictorial scales, graduating to 4-point by age 10. Show personal growth trajectories rather than peer comparisons. Add metacognitive prompts ("How well did you understand this?") that serve as both data collection and developmental training, following Kuhn's (2000) argument that enhancing metacognitive awareness is a core educational goal.

**Ages 10–13 (2do Ciclo Básica):** The transition to Piaget's formal operations begins — abstract reasoning, hypothetical thinking, and deliberate metacognition emerge. Harter documented two critical transitions: from over-positive to realistic self-view (precipitating **sharp self-esteem declines**), and from global to domain-specific self-concept. Self-Determination Theory research (Ryan & Deci, 2020) identifies this as a period of documented motivation decline during the elementary-to-middle school transition, buffered by autonomy-supportive environments. The ASRQ (Ryan & Connell, 1989), validated for this age, shows children can differentiate external, introjected, identified, and intrinsic motivation. Platform implication: offer domain-specific self-assessment (academic, social, physical), use 4-5 point Likert scales plus open reflection, and provide identity exploration features (interest inventories, goal-setting journals). Frame challenges as puzzles, not tests. Make sharing opt-in, protecting the vulnerable self-esteem of this stage.

**Ages 13–18 (Enseñanza Media):** Full formal operations enable "thinking about thinking itself." Erikson's identity-versus-role-confusion crisis drives experimentation with multiple selves across contexts. Harter showed adolescents describe themselves differently in different settings, initially experiencing this as fraudulence before achieving integration in late adolescence. Weil et al. (2013, Consciousness and Cognition) demonstrated that metacognitive ability shows a **prolonged developmental trajectory through adolescence**, peaking in late adolescence. Self-reporting becomes sophisticated but susceptible to social desirability bias — Harter's structured alternative format was specifically designed to counter this. Platform implication: use adult-like, clean interface design (teens reject "kiddie" aesthetics per NN/g). Provide rich self-reflection tools — journaling, strategy analysis, portfolio curation. Support identity exploration tied to Chile's humanistic-scientific vs. technical-professional tracks. Give students full control over what they share. Shift teachers from proxy reporters to coaches interpreting rich student-generated data.

| Age | Primary data source | Self-report capacity | Scale type | Interface mode |
|-----|-------------------|---------------------|------------|----------------|
| 3–5 | Behavioral/observational | Minimal; binary | 2 faces | Voice-narrated, large touch targets |
| 6–7 | Mixed: behavioral + simple self-report | Emerging | 2–3 point pictorial | Simple text + audio + icons |
| 8–10 | Self-report with scaffolding | Good reliability | 3–4 point Likert | Metacognitive prompts, choice menus |
| 10–13 | Self-report as primary | Strong, domain-specific | 4–5 point + open text | Identity tools, opt-in sharing |
| 13–18 | Sophisticated self-report | Adult-comparable | 5–7 point + narrative | Clean professional design, full autonomy |

## 3. Sociograms in schools are powerful but require careful methodology

Sociometric methodology has a 90-year evidence base beginning with Jacob Moreno's *Who Shall Survive?* (1934). The modern framework, established by Coie, Dodge & Coppotelli (1982), uses two independent dimensions — **social preference** (acceptance minus rejection) and **social impact** (acceptance plus rejection) — to classify students into five validated groups: popular, rejected, neglected, controversial, and average. This two-dimensional system, refined by García Bacete & Cillessen (2017, Frontiers in Psychology), provides a research-validated vocabulary for asesores to understand classroom dynamics far beyond simple "popularity."

Age-specific reliability is well-documented. Wu, Hart, Draper & Olsen (2001) found **test-retest reliability of 0.79** for peer nominations in preschoolers using picture-based methods. Elementary school (ages 6-12) is the "sweet spot" for sociometric assessment — children interact in stable classrooms for hours daily, making classroom-based sociometrics highly valid. In middle school, methodological challenges emerge: students rotate through different class configurations, and a study of 664 sixth graders found an alphabetical roster sequence effect requiring counterbalancing (Poulin & Dishion, 2008). In adolescence, Cillessen & Mayeux (2004) showed that the meaning of "popularity" bifurcates — sociometric popularity (well-liked) diverges from perceived popularity (high-status/visible) — requiring separate measurement.

On the question of **unlimited versus limited nominations**, research consensus strongly favors unlimited. Terry (2000), Babcock et al. (2014), and Gommans & Cillessen (2015) all confirmed unlimited nominations produce more reliable, valid, and normally distributed data. This is "particularly important for validity in larger reference groups and with older children" (Cillessen & Marks, 2017). For early childhood, limited nominations (3 peers) reduce cognitive burden.

The ethical question of **negative nominations** ("Who do you least like?") has generated decades of concern — but the evidence shows no harm. Hayvren & Hymel (1984) found no negative effects on preschoolers. Bell-Dolan, Foster & Christopher (1992) found no negative effects on elementary children. Mayeux, Underwood & Risser (2007, Merrill-Palmer Quarterly) conducted a multi-informant study finding children were not hurt by testing, most enjoyed it, and did not feel peers treated them differently afterward. However, school officials and parents sometimes object (Putallaz & Wasserman, 1989), and for a platform serving schools (not research contexts), **avoiding direct negative nominations is prudent**. Instead, derive isolation from the absence of positive nominations and use indirect task-based framing ("Who do you find it difficult to work with?") when negative data is essential.

Several digital sociogram tools exist. **Sometics** operates in 20+ languages including Spanish. **SociogramPlus** stores all data locally for GDPR compliance and includes a privacy mode. A **Google Workspace add-on** integrates with Google Classroom, adds emojis for early readers, and generates smart group recommendations. None, however, integrates sociometric assessment with longitudinal student development tracking or early warning systems — all are standalone tools.

**Design implication:** GENERA should implement age-differentiated methods: picture-based rating for early childhood, positive peer nominations with unlimited choices for elementary, and expanded grade-level nominations with perceived popularity measures for adolescents. Use positive nominations only as the default, deriving social isolation from absence of connections. Employ a multi-informant approach — peer nominations supplemented by teacher observations (strong for externalizing behaviors, weak for peer dynamics per Rauterkus et al., 2025) and student self-reports of belonging. Apply roster counterbalancing for middle school. And never display individual nomination data to asesores — only aggregate patterns, cluster visualization, and trend analysis.

## 4. Relational data must be a conversation tool, not a surveillance system

The tension between data visibility and trust is not merely a design challenge — it is the existential question for any platform that makes relational data visible to adults. Get this wrong, and the platform destroys the relationships it aims to support.

Nichols & Monea (2022, Phi Delta Kappan) distinguish between **vertical surveillance** (an authority figure actively observing, as in traditional teaching) and **horizontal surveillance/dataveillance** (algorithms analyzing data and pushing judgments). They warn: "Without careful and continuous reflection, any surveillance — horizontal or vertical — can damage community trust and student well-being." The data imperative — where collecting some data justifies collecting more — drove GoGuardian from a device management tool to behavioral surveillance of **25+ million students**, including monitoring outside school hours. A 2023 survey found 82% of K-12 students report being subject to some form of monitoring.

The developmental psychology evidence is decisive on how monitoring affects adolescent trust. Kerr & Stattin (2000) overturned decades of assumption by showing that what was thought to be effective "parental monitoring" was actually **adolescent voluntary disclosure** — parents who knew more did so because their children chose to tell them. Hawk et al. (2013) found that parental privacy invasion actually **predicted reduced parental knowledge over time**. More invasive monitoring yields less information, not more. This translates directly: asesores who use sociometric data as surveillance will receive less authentic input from students than those who use it as a basis for supportive conversation.

The restorative justice tradition offers a powerful alternative model. Circle processes establish confidentiality as a co-created norm, participation is voluntary, and students can be trained as circle keepers (Oakland trains hundreds of youth facilitators). RAND Corporation's evaluation of restorative practices in Pittsburgh found reduced suspensions and improved school climate (Augustine et al., 2018). The ASCA Ethical Standards (2022) for school counselors provide an applicable framework: maintain confidentiality, explain its limits in "developmentally appropriate terms," minimize disclosure when breaching confidentiality is necessary, and — critically — **collaborate with students on how disclosure occurs**.

**Design implication for GENERA:** Build three data visibility tiers. Students see their own connections (mutual nominations), class wellbeing metrics, and self-reflection spaces. Asesores see class-level sociograms, isolation alerts, and trend analysis — but **individual negative nomination data is never shown**, only aggregate patterns ("3 students in this class received zero positive nominations"). Administrators see fully anonymized school-wide metrics. Position the entire system as a "belonging support tool," not a "problem detection system." Include guided conversation protocols for asesores ("When you notice a student appears isolated, here's how to approach a supportive conversation"). Build structural constraints against mission creep: data minimization, no behavioral prediction algorithms, no automated push alerts (asesores actively consult the data), defined data lifecycles with automatic deletion, and annual transparency reports to the school community. For adolescents specifically, adopt a model where students can see their own social network data and choose to share insights with asesores — making them partners rather than subjects of surveillance.

## 5. Special needs as context that shapes everything, not a separate module

Universal Design for Learning (UDL) Guidelines 3.0, updated by CAST in 2024, provide the foundational framework. UDL is built on three brain networks (affective, recognition, strategic) and prescribes multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression. Guo & Wang (2025) report a **37.4% increase in academic achievement** in high-UDL environments, with greatest benefits for students with lower baseline engagement. The critical insight: UDL should be GENERA's **default operating logic**, not an add-on for identified students.

The neurodiversity-affirming research is equally clear. Harrison et al. (2024, University of Melbourne/SMART Technologies) found "few verified digital resources for teachers to understand and apply neurodiversity-affirming strategies" — a gap GENERA can fill. Multiple researchers (Chandler 2024, Prizant & Finch 2022, South et al. 2021) document that compliance-based approaches lead to **masking, which correlates with anxiety, depression, trauma, and suicidality** in autistic adults. IEP goals should focus on "supporting autonomy, self-advocacy, self-determination, and well-being" rather than deficit remediation (Dallman et al., 2022).

For ASD specifically, sociometric and SEL data require calibrated interpretation. Qualls & Corbett (2016, Vanderbilt) found standard diagnostic measures administered by adults may **not accurately capture peer-context social differences**. The SRS-2 lacks measurement invariance across ASD and non-ASD groups (Frazier et al., 2014) — the same scores have different meanings. An MDPI study (2025) found **38.6% of SEN students reported bullying** vs. 4.8% of non-SEN peers, and SEN students experiencing bullying were in more fragmented social network structures.

Chile's PIE program (Decreto Supremo Nº170/2009) distinguishes between Transitory SEN (specific learning disorders, TEL, TDA, borderline intellectual functioning) and Permanent SEN (intellectual disability, ASD, sensory disabilities), serving **183,373+ students nationally** with a maximum of 7 SEN students per classroom for subsidy purposes. The FUDEI documentation must be filed on the MINEDUC platform. IntegratePIE already automates FUDEI synchronization and PAEC protocols for autistic students.

**Design implication:** Implement a "context layer" architecture where SEN status functions as an interpretive lens applied across all data views, not a siloed module. When a teacher views a student's academic trajectory, SEN context should appear as a sidebar annotation. When sociometric data shows a student with ASD as peripheral, the platform should automatically surface that student's communication profile and suggest neurodiversity-informed interpretation — fewer reciprocal nominations may reflect communication style differences, not social distress. Compare against neurodivergent-normed baselines, not universal ones. Lead student profiles with strengths, interests, and preferences before areas of challenge — this is not cosmetic but shapes how educators approach intervention. Use neurodiversity-affirming language throughout ("social communication differences" not "social skills deficits"). Accommodate self-report instruments with simplified language, visual supports, and concrete scenarios. Integrate with MINEDUC's PIE platform and FUDEI requirements, and support the co-teaching model prescribed by Decreto 170 with shared planning spaces.

## 6. Early warning works best with multiple indicators — but different signals matter at different ages

The Early Warning Intervention and Monitoring System (EWIMS), developed by the American Institutes for Research, has the strongest evidence base. A randomized controlled trial of 73 high schools and **35,888 students** (Faria et al., 2017) found EWIMS reduced chronic absences and course failures. The Oregon implementation reduced chronic absenteeism from **13.42% to 5.00%** (Sepanik et al., 2021). The system uses the ABC framework — Attendance below 90%, Behavior (one or more suspensions), Coursework failures — validated primarily for **grades 6-12**. In elementary grades, Balfanz & Byrnes (2019) found ABC indicators "lack the predictive power to reliably identify future" dropout risk, requiring different early indicators for younger children.

The KiVa anti-bullying program (University of Turku, Finland) provides the most relevant anti-bullying evidence. A large-scale RCT of 8,237 youth across 78 schools found **consistent beneficial effects on 7 of 11 dependent variables** (Kärnä et al., 2011), with effect sizes of 0.24-0.40 in grade 4. Critically for GENERA, **KiVa has been trialed in Chile**: Gaete et al. (2017, Trials) conducted a 3-arm cluster RCT in Santiago with 1,495 students in grades 4-5 in vulnerable schools, and KiVa is now being implemented in Chile's Valparaíso and Metropolitan regions through the Fundación para la Convivencia Digital.

Research on predictive signals reveals age-differentiated patterns. Cook et al.'s (2010, APA) meta-analysis found bullies score extreme on externalizing behaviors while victims score high on internalizing (withdrawal, passivity). Physical bullying dominates in early childhood; relational and verbal bullying emerge in middle childhood; cyberbullying rises in adolescence. Younger children over-report bullying; adolescents under-report due to social desirability. Sociometric position is a strong predictor — a Frontiers in Communication systematic review (2022) found three patterns: bullying associated with popular status, rejected students bullying as a frustration response, and mixed positions.

NLP approaches show promise but require caution. A RoBERTa-based model achieved **90.18% accuracy** classifying depressive versus non-depressive social media posts, and LIWC research found first-person singular pronoun usage positively correlated with depression (Zimmermann et al., 2017). However, models trained on adult English-language social media data have **unknown validity for children's academic writing in Chilean Spanish**.

Chile's own framework matters. Ley Nº 20.536 (2011) on school violence requires prevention policies and intervention protocols. The PACES monitoring system (López, Ascorra, Bilbao et al., 2016) uses validated questionnaires for students, teachers, and parents based on models from Israel and California. The JUNAEB Habilidades para la Vida program monitors school climate in low-SES schools.

**Design implication:** Build age-differentiated indicator profiles. For ages 3-7, prioritize developmental milestone tracking, teacher observations of social engagement, and attendance patterns. For ages 8-12, add sociometric position changes over time, academic trajectory shifts, self-reported well-being, and KiVa-aligned victimization surveys. For ages 13-18, add course performance trends, attendance micro-patterns, digital engagement drops, and within-student language changes in journal entries. Always require **multi-indicator convergence** before generating alerts — no single data source should trigger a flag. Implement a human-in-the-loop model where the platform presents patterns for asesor review rather than making automated determinations. Embed Chilean-validated instruments (PACES, adapted School Victimization Scale) rather than importing untested international tools. Align with Convivencia Escolar requirements under Ley 20.536.

## 7. The student's story must travel with them — and Chile's new privacy law demands it

Longitudinal data continuity is a core challenge because most platforms are structured around classrooms or school years, not around students. **Branching Minds** provides the strongest existing model with student records that persist across campuses and years. **Storypark's** parent-owned portfolio that families retain free access to indefinitely represents the ideal for data ownership. **Infinite Campus** enables state-wide tracking when deployed as a state system. But no platform combines institutional longitudinal tracking with family/student co-ownership.

The xAPI (Experience API) standard, published as IEEE 9274.1.1 in October 2023, offers a technical foundation. Using "Actor-Verb-Object" statements stored in a Learning Record Store (LRS), xAPI can track experiences across any platform or even offline activities, building comprehensive learner profiles over time. This standardized, portable format aligns with data portability requirements and protects against vendor lock-in.

**Chile's new data protection law (Ley 21.719)**, published December 13, 2024 and effective **December 1, 2026**, transforms the landscape. Modeled on the EU GDPR, it creates Chile's first Data Protection Authority (Agencia de Protección de Datos Personales), introduces sanctions of **2-4% of annual revenue** for violations, mandates privacy by design, requires breach notification, establishes data portability rights, and explicitly regulates sensitive data including education and health records. GENERA must be designed for full compliance from launch.

Regarding asesor handoff, no widely standardized protocol exists. This is a significant gap. Best practices from platforms and research include: role-based rather than person-based access (tied to "5th grade asesor" rather than a named individual), structured handoff summaries generated when students transition, audit trails for every access event, and family notification when their child's asesor changes.

**Design implication:** Adopt xAPI/LRS as the underlying data architecture for longitudinal tracking. Build a "student story" portfolio structured around the student (not the classroom or school year), combining quantitative indicators with qualitative narrative. Implement dual ownership: the school is data controller for institutional purposes, but families have co-ownership with permanent access and structured export capability — preventing the catastrophic data loss that occurred when FreshGrade shut down in 2022, destroying **18+ million student portfolios**. Design asesor transition protocols that generate handoff documents including student strengths, active concerns, family dynamics, effective strategies, and goals in progress. Implement privacy-tiered access with time-decaying sensitivity — older behavioral incidents become less prominent over time. Build all of this for Ley 21.719 compliance from day one, including explicit consent management, purpose limitation, right to erasure, and the ability for families to review and update information being transferred.

## 8. No platform supports student-led rituals — this is GENERA's biggest innovation opportunity

A thorough search revealed **no purpose-built digital platform** for student-led circles, peer-facilitated discussions, or student-presented family meetings. This is the most significant gap in the market and GENERA's clearest innovation opportunity.

The closest precedents are general-purpose tools adapted for specific practices. **Flipgrid** (now discontinued as a standalone product, folded into Microsoft Teams in September 2024) was the nearest model of a student-as-primary-user sharing platform, with QR-code entry, video recording, and peer review. Its design principle of minimal friction access is directly applicable. **Seesaw** provides multimodal creation tools (drawing, voice recording, photo, video, text) designed for children as content creators, with teacher approval gates that progressively release control. Student-led conferences at **High Tech High** are well-documented but supported entirely through generic tools (Google Slides, Google Sites).

**Transparent Classroom** (Montessori) and **Montessori Compass** track individual learning paths respecting child-led philosophy, demonstrating that developmental progress (not grades) can be tracked through teacher observations feeding into student profiles. A study of Montessori educators in Barcelona and Istanbul found educators valued documentation capability but worried about the tool replacing observational presence.

The Interaction Design and Children (IDC) research field, with 20+ years of work led by Allison Druin (University of Maryland), provides essential principles for child-as-facilitator interfaces: large clear icons with minimal text for younger students, audio prompts for non-readers, progressive disclosure (show only what's needed at each step), built-in "undo" safety, and warm emotional design language.

**Design implication:** Build a "Circle Companion" (not a "Circle Replacement") with features organized as "before the circle" and "after the circle" — never "during the circle." Include: circle prompt generators (curated by teachers, increasingly by students), facilitation rotation schedules, post-circle brief reflections (one emoji or one word — not a survey), a ritual library where Equipo Base groups save favorite activities, and co-created agreements that the group revisits. For family presentations, build an end-to-end student-led process: guided preparation workflow for selecting growth artifacts, reflection prompts connecting to the personal growth plan, peer practice within Equipo Base, a simple presentation mode for the child, and family feedback post-presentation. Implement a progressive facilitation scaffold: Level 1 (youngest) — full step-by-step guide with adult co-facilitation; Level 2 — platform provides prompts and timing, student leads; Level 3 — student selects prompts from a bank; Level 4 — student designs their own ritual with the platform as optional aid. **The platform should actively discourage screen use during the circle itself** with explicit "screens down" prompts during relational moments.

## 9. Five pedagogical traditions converge on one principle: technology must be invisible

**Escola Virolai** (Barcelona) provides the most direct model for GENERA's core practices. Director Coral Regí has stated: "What we care most about is preparing children to make personal and professional decisions, coherent with their own values." Every primary student creates a "plan personal" with objectives based on two axes: **self-esteem and self-knowledge**. Students from age 3 take on classroom responsibilities ("encargos"), and primary delegates form a student assembly. Current director Sandra Entrena's key insight for GENERA: "We use technology to achieve learning objectives, but we never design our pedagogical proposals to fit the technology." Virolai uses Clickedu as their LMS, but technology is described as "invisible" — integrated ubiquitously but never the center. Every three weeks, the school sends parents indicators of each student's evolution.

**High Tech High** validates student-led conferences and portfolio-based documentation. Their published SLC guide states: "The student is in charge. The structure of the SLC helps empower students and builds a sense of responsibility and accountability." The key insight: SLCs should occur midway through grading periods (not at the end), giving students time to act on feedback. HTH portfolios are living documents updated throughout the year, tied to exhibitions with authentic audiences.

**Reggio Emilia and Harvard's Project Zero** (Making Learning Visible collaboration, 1997-2011) established that documentation is a pedagogical tool, not an assessment tool. Steve Seidel: "Documentation not only allows us to make visible the dynamics of individual and group learning, but also is itself a tool that can promote individual learning within the group." Howard Gardner: "Assessment of learning is not necessarily tests, and documentation need not be private." The critical principle: documentation should be shared back with learners so they can revisit and deepen their learning — it serves learning, not accountability.

**Japanese tokkatsu** (特別活動) is universally practiced in all Japanese schools and provides the closest structural parallel to GENERA's Equipo Base. The **han** (班) — small heterogeneous groups doing cooperative activities — maps directly. Nichoku (daily rotating monitors), kakari (student-chosen roles), and kaigi (class meetings for goal-setting and reflection) provide a complete model of student-led classroom life. Professor Ryoko Tsuneyoshi (University of Tokyo): "Twice-daily brief class meetings and longer weekly meetings, led by students, provide a forum for students to raise and solve issues." Tokkatsu has been successfully exported to Egypt, Indonesia, Peru, Iraq, and Malaysia. **It operates without any specific digital platform** — it is entirely analog, reinforcing that GENERA should be the thinnest possible digital layer over fundamentally human practices.

**Restorative justice circles** resist digitization in their core mechanics. The five essential elements — sitting in a circle, a centerpiece, a talking piece, co-created agreements, and predictable rhythm — are physical and relational. What digitizes well: prompt banks, agreement archives, facilitation schedules. What must not be digitized: the circle itself, the talking piece passing, the silence, the eye contact.

**Positive psychology** provides measurable frameworks. Geelong Grammar School (Australia) has implemented Seligman's PERMA-H model since 2008, with Student Action Plans where students outline targets for wellbeing, growth, and performance each term. The VIA Youth Survey (ages 8-17) measures 24 character strengths with reliability α > .70 across all scales (Park & Peterson, 2006). Adler et al. (2016) demonstrated that teaching wellbeing increases academic performance in Bhutan, Mexico, and Peru — directly relevant evidence for Chile.

The emerging research on **documentation versus presence** adds a critical caution. A 2025 article in TheSector warns: "When educators are too quick to capture images, the immediacy of the screen can interrupt the intimacy of the moment. We risk reducing rich experiences to snapshots." Krechevsky (Project Zero) emphasized: "Documentation is not an end in itself... Your response to 'What do I want to learn?' will help guide you in knowing what to do with the documentation."

**Design implication:** Seven meta-principles emerge from these traditions. First, the "thin digital layer" principle: the platform is the thinnest possible support over fundamentally human practices (from tokkatsu and Virolai). Second, the "before and after, not during" principle: digital tools prepare for and reflect on relational experiences but go silent during them (from circles and presence research). Third, the "progressive handover" principle: student ownership increases developmentally, with scaffolding progressively removed (from Virolai, tokkatsu, HTH). Fourth, the "documentation as learning" principle: every documentation prompt frames reflection ("What did you notice?"), not accountability ("What did you achieve?") (from Reggio/MLV). Fifth, the "student as primary user" principle: the child's interface is designed from scratch for the child, not simplified from the teacher's view (from IDC research, Seesaw). Sixth, the "strength-based architecture" principle: growth plans focus on developing strengths, not remediating deficits (from PERMA, VIA). Seventh, the "Chilean institutional alignment" principle: integrate with formación ciudadana (Ley 20.911) and convivencia escolar requirements so schools get compliance value alongside pedagogical value.

## 10. The pivot from subject to author happens around age 8-10 — and must be deliberately designed

Multiple theoretical frameworks converge on the same developmental moment. Robert Kegan's (1982, 1994) orders of consciousness place the shift at Order 2 (childhood, ages 6-12) when children construct "durable categories" and begin making meaning of their experience, transitioning to Order 3 (adolescence) where cross-categorical thinking and relational meaning-making emerge. True "self-authorship" in Kegan's sense is an Order 4 adult achievement — but the precursors are observable from approximately ages 8-10. Marcia Baxter Magolda (2001, Miami University) identified four phases from "following formulas" through "becoming the author of one's life," with the Learning Partnerships Model providing three principles: validating students as knowledge-creators, situating learning in students' experiences, and defining learning as mutually constructing meaning.

The IB system deliberately architectures this transition. The **PYP Exhibition** (Grade 5, age 10-12) is explicitly designed as the moment when students become authors of their learning story: "students demonstrate their ability to take responsibility for their learning." The **MYP Personal Project** (Grade 10, age 15-16) escalates autonomy to independent inquiry with a process journal and reflective report. The continuum — PYP Exhibition → MYP Personal Project → DP Extended Essay → CAS Portfolio — provides a clear model of progressively increasing student authorship.

Margaret Carr's Learning Stories framework from New Zealand provides the richest model for the early transition. Stories move from **teacher-authored** → **teacher-child dialogue** → **child-dictated** → **child-authored**. A 2019 study published in Early Years (Taylor & Francis) found that when teachers revisited portfolio books with children using a "reminiscing orientation," children talked more and used more complex language. The NAEYC book *Learning Stories and Teacher Inquiry Groups* (Escamilla et al., 2021) emphasizes "reclaiming the voices of teachers, children, and families."

Carol Anne Wien's (2011, ECRP) five-stage progression of Reggio-inspired documentation identifies Stage 4 — "making children's theories visible" — as the pivot point where documentation shifts from showing what happened to showing what children think. In Wien's case study, Grade 1 students actively engaged with documentation of their own theories, leading to theory revision through dialogue.

Helen Barrett (University of Alaska, REFLECT Initiative), the foremost ePortfolio researcher since the 1990s, offers a critical warning: **"If portfolios are required, their nature changes to one of an assessment management system"** and student voice is lost. She advocates for a dual architecture — learner-owned portfolio (assessment FOR learning) alongside institutional assessment data (assessment OF learning) — that must never collapse into a single system.

Platform evidence reinforces these insights. **Seesaw** uses teacher approval gates that progressively release control. **FreshGrade** (before its 2022 shutdown) designed separate teacher, student, and parent apps, with kindergartners saying "Can you put this on my portfolio so mommy and daddy can see it?" **Mahara** (open-source, University of Auckland) is learner-owned by architecture, with flexible sharing permissions and template-to-freedom progression. The Ontario Teachers' Federation documented kindergartners visiting other classes to model portfolio documentation with peers.

**Design implication:** GENERA should implement a deliberate, staged authorship transition with explicit metadata tracking who authored each entry. The visible ratio of child-authored to adult-authored entries should shift over time as a design feature:

| Chilean stage | Documentation author | Platform feature |
|---|---|---|
| Educación Parvularia (0-6) | Teacher/parent authors; child contributes voice | Learning Stories format; child selects photos, records audio |
| 1er Ciclo Básica (6-10) | Co-authorship emerging | Child creates artifacts, adds reflections; teacher structures |
| 2do Ciclo Básica — the pivot (~age 10) | Student becomes primary author | "Exhibition moment" — student designs, presents, community witnesses |
| Enseñanza Media (12-18) | Student fully owns narrative | Student-designed portfolio; teacher as consultant; public sharing controls |

Build a dual-layer architecture: a learner-facing portfolio that the student owns (assessment FOR learning) and an institutional data layer for programmatic reporting (assessment OF learning). Tag every entry along an authorship spectrum ("documented by educator / family / child / co-authored"). Include defined "exhibition moments" at developmental transitions — end of Parvularia, end of Básica, end of Media — where the documentation format formally shifts. Use open-source or Chilean-controlled infrastructure with full export capability to prevent the platform dependency that destroyed FreshGrade's portfolios.

## What these ten answers mean together

The research across these ten questions reveals GENERA is not merely adding features to an existing category — it is constructing a new category of educational technology. The closest existing platforms cover at most two of GENERA's three dimensions (individual development, social dynamics, early warning), none spans early childhood through graduation, and none embeds the asesor relationship or student-led ritual practices as first-class design elements.

Five cross-cutting principles should govern every design decision. **Technology must be invisible** — the platform supports human relationships; it never replaces them (Virolai, tokkatsu, restorative circles). **The student progressively becomes the author** — from subject of adult documentation to author of their own growth story, with a deliberate pivot around age 8-10 (Kegan, Carr, IB PYP). **Data serves belonging, not surveillance** — sociometric data is a conversation tool for asesores, never a monitoring system, with structural constraints against mission creep (Kerr & Stattin, Nichols & Monea, ASCA). **Neurodiversity shapes interpretation, not separation** — SEN status functions as context that enriches understanding of all other data, with strengths-first architecture and affirming language (UDL 3.0, Harrison et al.). **Chilean law and culture are not afterthoughts** — Ley 21.719 (effective December 2026), Decreto 170 (PIE), Ley 20.536 (convivencia), and Ley 20.911 (formación ciudadana) must be embedded in the platform architecture from day one, not bolted on later.

GENERA's distinctive opportunity lies precisely in the integration that no other platform has achieved: developmental tracking, social dynamics mapping, early warning detection, and family co-participation across the entire school journey — built on the "thin digital layer" principle that keeps the human practices at the center. The research is clear that this integration is possible, needed, and — if executed with the ethical safeguards and developmental sensitivity the evidence demands — transformative for Chilean schools.
